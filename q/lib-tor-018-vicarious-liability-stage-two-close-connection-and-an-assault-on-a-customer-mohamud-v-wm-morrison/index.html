Kwame is employed by Fuelstop Ltd as a cashier at a petrol station kiosk in England. His job is to serve customers and deal with their questions. A customer, Mr Varga, asks politely whether he can print some documents. Kwame swears at him and orders him to leave. Mr Varga walks back to his car on the forecourt. Kwame follows him, opens the passenger door, tells him never to come back to 'my' petrol station and punches him repeatedly. Fuelstop Ltd had never authorised violence and had no reason to think Kwame was dangerous.

Which of the following best describes whether Fuelstop Ltd is vicariously liable for the assault?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer D: It is liable, because Kwame was acting within the field of activities entrusted to him in dealing with a customer, and the assault was part of an unbroken sequence of events closely connected with his job.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

An employer is vicariously liable where the employee's wrong is so closely connected with the field of activities entrusted to him that it is right to hold the employer liable.

Vicarious liability stage two: close connection and an assault on a customer (Mohamud v WM Morrison)

It is accepted that Kwame was Fuelstop's employee, so the question is the second stage: whether there was a sufficient connection between the position in which he was employed and his wrongful conduct to make it right for the employer to be held liable. In Mohamud v WM Morrison Supermarkets plc [2016] UKSC 11, on very similar facts, the Supreme Court asked what functions or field of activities had been entrusted to the employee, viewed broadly, and then whether the wrongful conduct was sufficiently connected with them. Kwame's job was to attend to customers and respond to their inquiries. His abusive response was inexcusable but within that field of activities, and what followed, including following Mr Varga to the forecourt and ordering him never to return, was an unbroken sequence of events in which he was purporting to act about his employer's business. The employer is therefore vicariously liable, without any need to show fault on its part.

Why A is wrong: The field of activities is viewed broadly; misconduct in the course of dealing with customers can be closely connected with the job.

Why B is wrong: In Mohamud, following the customer onto the forecourt did not break the connection; it was an unbroken sequence of events.

Why C is wrong: Vicarious liability is a form of strict liability for the employee's tort; it does not depend on the employer's own negligence.

Why E is wrong: Liability requires a close connection between the job and the wrong, not merely that the wrong occurred during working hours.
