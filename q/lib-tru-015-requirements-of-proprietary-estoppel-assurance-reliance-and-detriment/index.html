Over fifteen years Bryn Hughes, who farmed in Herefordshire, England, repeatedly told his nephew Owain, 'Stick with me and this farm will be yours when I'm gone.' Relying on this, Owain worked full time on the farm for well below the going wage and turned down two better-paid jobs elsewhere. Nothing was ever put in writing and there was no contract. Shortly before he died, Bryn made a new will leaving the farm to a neighbour. Owain consults you.

Which of the following best describes Owain's position?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer D: He may have a claim in proprietary estoppel, because Bryn gave him an assurance about the farm on which he reasonably relied to his detriment, and the court may grant a remedy to prevent Bryn's estate going back on it.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

Proprietary estoppel arises where an assurance about property is reasonably relied on to the claimant's detriment, making it unconscionable for the promisor to go back on it.

Requirements of proprietary estoppel: assurance, reliance and detriment

Proprietary estoppel allows equity to intervene where a person makes a promise or assurance about property and the other relies on it to his detriment. Lord Briggs in Guest v Guest explained that a promise is not enforceable unless it is part of a contract, and a person is free to change his will until he dies. Even so, equity may give the promisee a remedy if a promise to confer property in the future was made and he acted to his detriment in reliance on it. Thorner v Major identifies the elements as an assurance, reasonable reliance and resulting detriment. Owain received repeated assurances that the farm would be his, and he worked for low wages and gave up better jobs in reliance on them. Those facts can satisfy each element, so an equity may arise against Bryn's estate. The remedy is for the court to decide in the light of all the circumstances, and is not guaranteed to be the whole farm.

Why A is wrong: Proprietary estoppel operates outside contract, so the absence of a signed written contract does not bar it.

Why B is wrong: There was no contract: Bryn's words were an informal family assurance, and a contract to dispose of land would in any case need signed writing.

Why C is wrong: That is the general rule, but equity intervenes where the promisee has relied on the assurance to his detriment.

Why E is wrong: Expectation is the usual starting point, but the remedy is discretionary and the court is not bound to give effect to the promise exactly.
