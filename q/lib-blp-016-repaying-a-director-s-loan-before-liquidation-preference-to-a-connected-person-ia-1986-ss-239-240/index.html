Repaying a director's loan before liquidation: preference to a connected person (IA 1986 ss.239-240)

Corvan Displays Ltd went into insolvent creditors' voluntary liquidation on 1 September 2026. Eighteen months earlier, when it was already unable to pay its debts as they fell due, it repaid in full an unsecured loan of £40,000 made to it by its managing director, Felix, while its trade creditors went unpaid. Felix says the company repaid him only because he had been pressing hard for payment, and not to put him in a better position than other creditors. The liquidator is considering whether the repayment can be challenged.

Can the liquidator challenge the repayment to Felix?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer A: Yes, as a preference, because it was given to a connected person within two years before the onset of insolvency while the company was unable to pay its debts, and a desire to prefer him is presumed unless he shows otherwise.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

A preference to a connected person within two years before the onset of insolvency, while the company is unable to pay its debts, can be set aside; desire to prefer is presumed (IA 1986 ss.239-240).

Repaying a director's loan before liquidation: preference to a connected person (IA 1986 ss.239-240)

A company gives a preference if it does something that puts a creditor in a better position, in the event of its insolvent liquidation, than the creditor would otherwise have been in (s.239(4) IA 1986). Repaying Felix in full while trade creditors went unpaid does this. For a person connected with the company otherwise than only as an employee, the relevant time is the two years ending with the onset of insolvency (s.240(1)(a)). As a director, Felix is connected, and the payment 18 months before liquidation falls within that period. The company was also unable to pay its debts at the time (s.240(2)). The court may only order relief if the company was influenced by a desire to prefer (s.239(5)). However, where the preference is given to a connected person, that desire is presumed unless the contrary is shown (s.239(6)). Felix bears the burden of rebutting the presumption, and saying that he was pressing for payment may not be enough.

Why B is wrong: The six-month period applies only to unconnected persons; for a connected person such as a director it is two years (s.240(1)(a)).

Why C is wrong: Paying a genuine existing debt is the typical way of giving a preference, because it improves that creditor's position in an insolvent liquidation.

Why D is wrong: Repaying a debt discharges a liability of equal value, so it is not a transaction at an undervalue; the correct challenge is as a preference.

Why E is wrong: Dishonesty is not an element of a preference claim; the test is a desire to prefer, which is presumed for connected persons.
