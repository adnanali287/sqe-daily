Litigation privilege: contemplated litigation and dominant purpose (SFO v ENRC; Three Rivers (No 6))

A fire damaged Orion Storage Ltd's warehouse in Swindon. Two days later, Orion's operations director commissioned an independent fire investigator to identify the cause so that Orion could improve its safety procedures. At that time no one had suggested any claim and none was contemplated. Report 1 was delivered a month later. Three months after the fire, a tenant, Velasquez Textiles Ltd, sent a letter of claim alleging that Orion's negligence caused the fire. Orion's solicitors then instructed a fire engineer to report on liability so that they could advise Orion on defending the threatened claim (Report 2). Velasquez has now issued proceedings and seeks inspection of both reports.

Which of the following best describes whether the reports are protected by litigation privilege?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer D: Report 1 is not privileged, because litigation was not in contemplation and its dominant purpose was not the conduct of litigation; Report 2 is protected by litigation privilege.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

Litigation privilege protects communications with third parties only if litigation was in progress or reasonably contemplated and the communication's sole or dominant purpose was conducting that adversarial litigation.

Litigation privilege: contemplated litigation and dominant purpose (SFO v ENRC; Three Rivers (No 6))

In SFO v ENRC [2018] EWCA Civ 2006, para 64, the Court of Appeal restated Lord Carswell's test in Three Rivers DC v Bank of England (No 6) [2004] UKHL 48. Communications between parties or their solicitors and third parties for the purpose of obtaining information or advice in connection with existing or contemplated litigation are privileged only when litigation is in progress or in contemplation, the communications were made for the sole or dominant purpose of conducting that litigation, and the litigation is adversarial. When Report 1 was commissioned, no claim had been suggested or contemplated, and its purpose was operational: finding the cause in order to improve safety. It fails the first two conditions, so it is not privileged and, if relevant, must be disclosed and is open to inspection. Report 2 was commissioned by solicitors after a letter of claim, for the dominant purpose of advising on defending that claim. It is protected by litigation privilege. Orion would lose that protection only if it chose to rely on the report as expert evidence.

Why A is wrong: A connection with the subject matter is not enough; litigation must be in contemplation and the dominant purpose must be the litigation.

Why B is wrong: Litigation privilege specifically covers communications with third parties, such as experts, made for the dominant purpose of contemplated litigation.

Why C is wrong: Later usefulness does not create privilege; the test is applied to the purpose and circumstances when the document was created.

Why E is wrong: A privileged expert report need not be disclosed unless the party chooses to rely on it; CPR 35.13 concerns reports a party wishes to use.
