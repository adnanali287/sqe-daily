Adverse inferences from silence and access to legal advice (CJPOA 1994 s.34(2A))

Kwame is arrested on suspicion of assault occasioning actual bodily harm after a fight outside a nightclub and taken to a police station, where he asks to speak to a solicitor. A superintendent authorises a delay in his access to legal advice, and officers interview him under caution before he has had any opportunity to consult a solicitor. Kwame answers 'no comment' to every question. At his trial, he gives evidence that he acted in self-defence. The prosecution invites the court to draw an adverse inference from his failure to mention self-defence in interview.

Which of the following best describes whether an adverse inference may be drawn under s.34 of the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer E: No inference may be drawn, because Kwame was at an authorised place of detention and had not been allowed an opportunity to consult a solicitor before being questioned.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

No adverse inference may be drawn under s.34 CJPOA 1994 from silence at a police station if the suspect had not been allowed an opportunity to consult a solicitor before being questioned (s.34(2A)).

Adverse inferences from silence and access to legal advice (CJPOA 1994 s.34(2A))

Section 34(1)(a) of the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994 allows the court or jury to draw such inferences as appear proper where the accused, on being questioned under caution before charge, failed to mention a fact later relied on in his defence which he could reasonably have been expected to mention. However, s.34(2A) provides that, where the accused was at an authorised place of detention at the time of the failure, subsections (1) and (2) do not apply if he had not been allowed an opportunity to consult a solicitor before being questioned. A police station is an authorised place of detention (s.38(2A)). Kwame was interviewed at a police station before he had any opportunity to consult a solicitor, so no s.34 inference may be drawn.

Why A is wrong: Whether the fact could reasonably have been expected to be mentioned is irrelevant once s.34(2A) disapplies subsections (1) and (2).

Why B is wrong: S.34(2A) turns on whether he had been allowed an opportunity to consult a solicitor, not on whether the denial of that opportunity was lawful.

Why C is wrong: S.34(1)(a) expressly covers questioning under caution before charge, as well as failures on charge under s.34(1)(b).

Why D is wrong: No inference is available here, and in any event s.38(3) provides that a person shall not be convicted solely on an inference drawn from such a failure.
