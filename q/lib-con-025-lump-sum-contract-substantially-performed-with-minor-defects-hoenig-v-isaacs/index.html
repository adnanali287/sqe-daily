Lucia Romano, an interior designer, agreed to design and fit out a studio flat in England for Owen Blake for a lump sum of £15,000, payable "£5,000 on starting, balance on completion". She completed all the work and Owen moved in and has used the furniture and fittings since. A built-in bookcase is 5 cm too short and a wardrobe door is warped; putting both right will cost £900. Owen has paid only the first £5,000 and refuses to pay anything more, saying Lucia has not performed the contract.

Which of the following best describes Owen's liability?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer D: Owen must pay the balance of £10,000 less £900, because the contract was substantially performed and he can set off the cost of remedying the defects.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

Where a lump sum contract is substantially performed, the contractor recovers the price less the cost of remedying defects; only a breach going to the root of the contract defeats the claim.

Lump sum contract substantially performed with minor defects (Hoenig v Isaacs)

In Hoenig v Isaacs the Court of Appeal held that, where a contract provides for a lump sum on completion of specified work, the courts lean against a construction that would deprive the contractor of payment simply because there are some defects or omissions. Denning LJ said the promise to complete is a term but not a condition: unless the breach goes to the root of the contract, such as abandoning the work half done, the employer cannot resist payment. He must pay and bring a cross-claim for the defects, or set them up in diminution of the price, usually measured by the cost of making them good. Lucia finished the work, Owen has used it, and the defects cost £900 to remedy against a £15,000 price. The contract was substantially performed. Owen must pay the £10,000 balance less £900, so £9,100.

Why A is wrong: The court leans against treating entire, defect-free performance as a condition precedent in a lump sum contract unless clear words require it.

Why B is wrong: Quantum meruit arises only where the contract price cannot be claimed, for example where performance fails at the root; here it was substantial.

Why C is wrong: Substantial performance entitles the contractor to the price, but subject to a deduction for the cost of putting right the defects.

Why E is wrong: Only a breach going to the root of the contract would justify refusing payment; minor defects in completed work do not.
