The money laundering reporting officer of Penrose Achebe LLP, an SRA-authorised firm in Birmingham, has made a suspicious activity report to the National Crime Agency about funds a client, Lukas Becker, wanted to use to buy shares in a private company. The firm did not proceed. A week later Lukas tells his solicitor, Grace Penrose, that he plans to put the same funds through a different purchase. Grace tells him: 'We have had to report our concerns about where this money came from. If you go ahead with this, you will be committing an offence.' She says this solely to persuade him not to go ahead.

Has Grace committed the offence of tipping off under s.333A of the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer C: No, because a professional legal adviser does not commit the offence where the disclosure is to her client and is made to dissuade the client from engaging in conduct amounting to an offence.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

A professional legal adviser does not commit tipping off by disclosing a report to her own client where the purpose is to dissuade the client from engaging in conduct amounting to an offence (POCA s.333D(2)).

Tipping off: telling a client of a report to dissuade an offence (POCA ss.333A and 333D(2))

Section 333A(1) makes it an offence, where information came to a person in the course of business in the regulated sector, to disclose that a disclosure has been made to the NCA or a nominated officer, where that is likely to prejudice any investigation. Grace's statement to Lukas reveals that a report was made, so on its face it is within s.333A. However, s.333D(2) provides that a professional legal adviser does not commit an offence under s.333A if the disclosure is to the adviser's client and is made for the purpose of dissuading the client from engaging in conduct amounting to an offence. Grace, a solicitor, spoke to her own client solely to persuade him not to launder the funds through another purchase, so the exception applies and she has not committed the offence.

Why A is wrong: Section 333D(2) creates a specific exception for a legal adviser's disclosure to her client to dissuade the client from an offence, so purpose matters.

Why B is wrong: NCA consent to the original transaction is irrelevant to whether this disclosure falls within the s.333D(2) exception.

Why D is wrong: Section 333A applies to any person who makes the disclosure, not only the nominated officer.

Why E is wrong: Disclosure to the person under suspicion is the paradigm case s.333A catches; it is saved here only by s.333D(2).
