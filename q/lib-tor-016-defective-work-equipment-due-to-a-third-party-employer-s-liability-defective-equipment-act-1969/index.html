Oakridge Joinery Ltd buys a set of power saws from a reputable manufacturer and checks them on delivery, as a reasonable employer would. One saw has a hidden manufacturing defect which no reasonable inspection would have revealed. While using the saw in the course of his employment at Oakridge's workshop in England, Darius, an employee, is injured when the blade guard fails because of the defect. Oakridge argues that it was not at fault and that Darius should sue the manufacturer.

Which of the following best describes Oakridge's liability to Darius?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer B: Oakridge is liable, because Darius's injury is deemed to be attributable to Oakridge's negligence, although Oakridge may seek contribution or an indemnity from the manufacturer.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

Where an employee is injured by defective work equipment and the defect is due to a third party's fault, the injury is deemed attributable to the employer's negligence (EL(DE)A 1969 s.1).

Defective work equipment due to a third party (Employer's Liability (Defective Equipment) Act 1969)

An employer's common law primary duty includes providing adequate plant and equipment. Section 1(1) of the Employer's Liability (Defective Equipment) Act 1969 strengthens this. Where an employee suffers personal injury in the course of employment because of a defect in equipment provided by the employer for its business, and the defect is attributable wholly or partly to the fault of a third party (whether identified or not), the injury is deemed to be also attributable to the employer's negligence. This applies whether or not the employer would be liable apart from the section. The defect in the saw resulted from the manufacturer's fault, so Oakridge is deemed negligent and is liable to Darius, even though it acted reasonably. The section preserves the employer's remedies by way of contribution or in contract against the manufacturer or supplier, and any contributory negligence by Darius. Under s.1(2) any agreement purporting to exclude or limit this liability is void.

Why A is wrong: Reasonable care in buying and inspecting is no answer, because s.1(1) deems the injury attributable to the employer's negligence.

Why C is wrong: The deeming provision applies whether or not the third party is identified or solvent.

Why D is wrong: The 1969 Act reversed the position that buying from a reputable supplier discharged the employer's duty as to defective equipment.

Why E is wrong: Section 1(2) makes void any agreement purporting to exclude or limit the employer's liability under s.1(1).
