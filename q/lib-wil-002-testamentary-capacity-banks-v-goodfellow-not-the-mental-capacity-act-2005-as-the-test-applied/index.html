Winifred, aged 88, made a will in 2025 leaving her whole estate to her carer, Precious, and nothing to her son, Lewis. Eight months earlier she had been diagnosed with mild dementia. She has now died, domiciled in England. Lewis has entered a caveat and intends to challenge the will on the ground that Winifred lacked testamentary capacity. He asks you which legal test the court will apply to decide that question.

Which of the following best describes the test the court will apply?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer A: The common law test in Banks v Goodfellow: whether Winifred understood the nature and effect of making a will, the extent of her property and the claims to which she ought to give effect, with no disorder of mind poisoning her affections or perverting her sense of right.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

Testamentary capacity is tested by Banks v Goodfellow: understanding the nature and effect of the will, the extent of the property and the claims of others, free from any disorder of mind distorting the dispositions.

Testamentary capacity: Banks v Goodfellow, not the Mental Capacity Act 2005, as the test applied

The courts continue to determine testamentary capacity by the common law test in Banks v Goodfellow (1870) LR 5 QB 549. In Clitheroe v Bond [2021] EWHC 1102 (Ch), Falk J set out the test: the testator must understand the nature of the act and its effects, understand the extent of the property being disposed of, be able to comprehend and appreciate the claims to which they ought to give effect, and no disorder of the mind may poison their affections, pervert their sense of right or prevent the exercise of their natural faculties. She stated that, had the point been open on appeal, she would have concluded that the Banks test continues to apply rather than the Mental Capacity Act 2005 test. A diagnosis of mild dementia is relevant evidence, but the question is whether Winifred met the Banks requirements when she made the will. Where there is real doubt about capacity, the burden of proof ultimately lies on the person propounding the will (here Precious).

Why B is wrong: The High Court in Clitheroe v Bond (following earlier first-instance decisions) held that the MCA 2005 has not displaced the Banks v Goodfellow test for testamentary capacity.

Why C is wrong: A diagnosis is evidence only; a person with mild dementia may still satisfy the Banks test at the time the will is made.

Why D is wrong: A testator with capacity may make an unwise or unfair will; the test concerns understanding, not the reasonableness of the gifts.

Why E is wrong: This is a civil question decided on the balance of probabilities, and the legal burden ultimately rests on the propounder of the will, not on Lewis to a criminal standard.
