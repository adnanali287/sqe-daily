Fenbrook Chemicals Ltd stores large quantities of a highly corrosive industrial solvent in tanks on its site in England, an extraordinary use of land carrying an increased danger to neighbours if the solvent escapes. Despite Fenbrook having taken all reasonable care, a tank fails and the solvent escapes onto the neighbouring farm owned and occupied by Mr Lindqvist. It ruins crops and contaminates his land. Mr Lindqvist also suffers burns to his hands when trying to stop the flow. Damage of this kind from an escape was foreseeable.

Which of the following best describes Mr Lindqvist's claim under the rule in Rylands v Fletcher?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer C: He can recover for the damage to his land and crops without proving negligence, but not for his burns, which cannot be claimed under Rylands.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

An occupier who keeps an exceptionally dangerous thing in extraordinary circumstances is strictly liable for foreseeable damage to a neighbour's property from its escape, but not for personal injury.

Rylands v Fletcher: strict liability for property damage but not personal injury

Under the rule in Rylands v Fletcher, as restated in Transco plc v Stockport MBC and summarised in Stannard v Gore [2012] EWCA Civ 1248, an occupier who brings or keeps on its land an exceptionally dangerous or mischievous thing in extraordinary or unusual circumstances is liable, without proof of negligence, for damage to a neighbouring occupier's property interest caused by the thing's escape, subject to limited defences. Fenbrook kept large quantities of a corrosive solvent in a way the facts describe as an extraordinary use carrying increased danger; the solvent escaped onto neighbouring land and caused foreseeable damage. Mr Lindqvist, as owner-occupier, can therefore recover for the damage to his land and crops even though Fenbrook took all reasonable care. However, Rylands is a species of nuisance protecting interests in land, so a claim for death or personal injury cannot be brought under it. His burns must be claimed in negligence, which would require proof of fault.

Why A is wrong: Personal injury is not recoverable under Rylands v Fletcher, which protects rights in or enjoyment of land.

Why B is wrong: Rylands liability is strict; the claimant need not prove negligence.

Why D is wrong: Physical damage to land and crops is damage to property, not pure economic loss, and it is what Rylands protects; the burns are not recoverable under it.

Why E is wrong: The facts state that this storage was an extraordinary use carrying increased danger, so it was not an ordinary use of land.
