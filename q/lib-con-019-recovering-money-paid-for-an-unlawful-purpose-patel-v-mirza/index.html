Ollie Grant paid £25,000 to his friend Dev Rana, a trader, so that Dev could place bets on the share price of a listed company using inside information Dev expected to receive. Both knew that dealing on inside information is a criminal offence. The expected information never came, Dev placed no bets, and he has refused to return the money. Ollie sues Dev in England to recover the £25,000.

Which of the following best describes whether Ollie can recover the £25,000?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer C: Yes, because a claimant who meets the ordinary requirements of unjust enrichment is not barred merely because the money was paid for an unlawful purpose, and nothing here makes denial of the claim proportionate.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

Illegality bars a claim only where enforcing it would harm the integrity of the legal system, judged by the prohibition's purpose, other policies and proportionality; restitution of money paid for an unlawful purpose is normally allowed.

Recovering money paid for an unlawful purpose (Patel v Mirza)

In Patel v Mirza the Supreme Court replaced the reliance test with a policy-based approach to illegality. Lord Toulson said the rationale is that it would be contrary to the public interest to enforce a claim if that would harm the integrity of the legal system. The court considers the underlying purpose of the prohibition transgressed, any other relevant public policy, and whether denying the claim would be a proportionate response to the illegality. On facts closely resembling these, the court held that a claimant who satisfies the ordinary requirements of a claim in unjust enrichment should not be debarred from enforcing it by reason only that the money was paid for an unlawful purpose. Dev was enriched by £25,000 at Ollie's expense, and the basis for the payment failed. Returning the money does not enforce the illegal bargain, so Ollie can recover it.

Why A is wrong: Patel v Mirza rejected the reliance test from Tinsley v Milligan in favour of the policy-based approach.

Why B is wrong: The purpose for which the money was paid wholly failed when no bets were placed; that is the basis of the unjust enrichment claim, not a bar to it.

Why D is wrong: The court rejected a rigid rule leaving the loss where it falls; whether to deny the claim depends on the policy factors and proportionality.

Why E is wrong: Claiming lost profits would enforce the illegal agreement itself; Ollie's claim is limited to restoring what he paid.
