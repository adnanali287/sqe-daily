Sterling Vault Ltd, an online dealer in England, sold silver coins to members of the public on an 'unallocated' basis. Its terms said the coins were 'stored and insured on your behalf', but no coins were ever set aside or identified for any customer, and the company remained free to buy coins from any source to meet delivery requests. The company is now insolvent and its bank holds a valid floating charge over all its assets, which has crystallised. Its remaining stock of silver coins is far smaller than the total quantity customers paid for. The customers argue that the company holds its remaining coins on trust for them.

Which of the following best describes the customers' position in respect of the remaining coins?

Choose one answer to see the reasoning.

Worked answer

Correct answer C: They have no proprietary interest in any of the coins, because no coins were ever identified as theirs, so any trust failed for want of certain subject matter.

THE RULE TO REMEMBER

A trust of goods requires certain subject matter: where goods are unascertained and never appropriated to the buyer, no legal or equitable proprietary interest arises.

Certainty of subject matter: unallocated generic goods (Re Goldcorp)

An express trust needs certainty of subject matter: it must be possible to identify the property the trust attaches to. In Re Goldcorp the Privy Council held that buyers of unallocated bullion acquired no proprietary interest, legal or equitable, because no bullion had ever been ascertained and appropriated to their contracts. The seller's freedom to source the goods anywhere meant the sales were of generic goods, not goods from an identified bulk. Even assuming the company intended a trust, there was never a separate stock to which it could attach, and the remaining stock was simply another company asset. Applied here, no coins were set aside for any customer and Sterling Vault could meet orders from any source. The promise that coins were 'stored on your behalf' cannot create a title to goods nobody can identify. The customers are therefore unsecured creditors with personal claims only, and the bank's crystallised charge takes priority over the remaining coins.

Why A is wrong: A proportionate share in a shifting stock is exactly what Goldcorp rejected: the contracts were for generic goods, not a share of an identified bulk.

Why B is wrong: The Board refused to impose a lien or remedial proprietary right to defeat the bank's valid security.

Why D is wrong: Order of payment is irrelevant where no customer ever acquired any proprietary interest in the coins.

Why E is wrong: Calling the company a fiduciary adds nothing: a fiduciary relationship cannot create a proprietary interest where there is no identifiable property.
