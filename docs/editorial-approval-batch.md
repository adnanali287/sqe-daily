## Guides

### `guides.how-to-answer-sqe1-single-best-answer-questions`

Fingerprint: `6df4c3226083`

- **Title**: How to answer SQE1 single best answer questions
- **Meta description**: A step-by-step method for SQE1 single best answer questions: read the question first, name the issue, rule options out and keep to time.
- **Heading**: How to answer SQE1 single best answer questions

> Every SQE1 question is a single best answer question: a short scenario, a question and five options. More than one option may look plausible, but only one is the best answer to the question asked. This guide sets out a simple method you can use on every question, and how to keep to time on the day.

**Know the format**

> Each question has three parts. The scenario sets out the facts. The question, sometimes called the lead-in, tells you exactly what you are being asked: the legal position, the next step or the best advice. Five options follow. From January 2027, each FLK assessment has 170 questions, answered in two sessions of 85 questions, with 2 hours 33 minutes for each session. You can try real examples in the SRA's [sample questions](https://sqe.sra.org.uk/assessments/sqe1-assessments/sqe1-sample-questions).

**Step 1: read the question first**

> Read the final sentence before the facts. Knowing whether you are asked for the likely outcome, the correct procedure or the most appropriate advice tells you what to look for as you read the scenario.

**Step 2: name the issue**

> Read the scenario once, carefully. Note who is involved, the dates, sums and deadlines, and any detail that seems deliberately placed. Most scenarios turn on one legal issue. Try to name it, and the rule that governs it, before you look at the options.

**Step 3: answer before you look**

> Decide what you think the answer is, then look for the option that matches it. This stops plausible but wrong options from steering your reasoning.

**Step 4: rule options out**

> If no option matches, work through them one at a time. Rule out any option that misstates the law, answers a different question or depends on facts that are not in the scenario. Watch for options that are true statements of law but do not answer the question asked.

**Step 5: choose the best answer**

> Sometimes two options both look right. Go back to the question: the best answer is the one that most fully and precisely answers what was asked, on these facts. If you are still unsure, make your best choice and move on rather than losing time.

**Keep to time**

> Two hours 33 minutes for 85 questions is about 1 minute 48 seconds a question. Some questions will take less time than that. If one is taking much longer, make your best choice and move on.

**Practise the method**

> The method becomes automatic with practice. Use the [daily question](/) to rehearse it: read the question, name the issue, answer before you look, then read the worked explanation for every option, including the ones you ruled out.

### `guides.sqe1-revision-timetable`

Fingerprint: `e4c3ae66bfee`

- **Title**: SQE1 revision timetable: how to plan the weeks to your exam
- **Meta description**: A flexible SQE1 revision timetable: work back from your FLK1 date, cover every subject, build in daily practice and leave time to review.
- **Heading**: SQE1 revision timetable

> There is no single right way to revise for SQE1. Your timetable depends on how long you have, what you already know and what else is happening in your life. This guide shows one way to build a plan that covers every subject, builds in regular practice and leaves time to review before the exam.

**Start from your dates**

> Write down your FLK1 and FLK2 dates and count back. For the January 2027 sitting, FLK1 runs from 11 to 15 January 2027 and FLK2 from 18 to 22 January 2027. Check [SQE1 exam dates](/sqe1-exam-dates/) and the SRA’s own pages for your booking deadline. Then decide how many hours a week you can realistically give to revision.

**List what you need to cover**

> Use the SRA's [SQE1 assessment specification](https://sqe.sra.org.uk/assessments/sqe1-assessments/sqe1-specification) as your checklist. It sets out the subjects in each paper and what is assessed in each. Rate each area as strong, workable or weak. That rating, not the order of the specification, should decide where your time goes.

**Plan in three phases**

> Learn: work through each subject, giving more time to your weaker areas. Practise: once you have covered a subject, answer questions on it regularly, and keep returning to earlier subjects so they stay fresh. Review: in the final weeks, stop adding new material and focus on mixed practice, timed sessions and the rules you keep getting wrong.

**A sample 16-week outline**

> If you have about 16 weeks before FLK1, one approach is: weeks 1 to 6 on FLK1 subjects; weeks 7 to 12 on FLK2 subjects; weeks 13 to 15 on mixed practice and timed sessions across both papers; and week 16 for light review and rest. Because FLK2 comes a week after FLK1, keep some FLK2 practice going in the final weeks. Treat this as a starting point and adjust it to your weak areas and commitments.

**Make practice a daily habit**

> Short, regular practice spreads your revision out and keeps earlier subjects in play. A [daily question](/) takes a few minutes. After you answer, read the explanation for every option and note the rule to remember. Our guide to [answering single best answer questions](/guides/how-to-answer-sqe1-single-best-answer-questions/) sets out a method to rehearse each time.

**Practise at exam length**

> From January 2027, each FLK assessment is sat in two sessions of 85 questions, each lasting 2 hours 33 minutes, and each session covers a set group of subjects. Before the exam, do some practice sessions of that length so the pace feels familiar.

**Look after yourself**

> Build rest days into the plan and protect your sleep in the final week. A timetable you can keep is better than an ambitious one you abandon.

> Land law is assessed in FLK2. It covers estates and interests in land, registered and unregistered land, co-ownership and trusts of land, leases, easements, freehold covenants and mortgages. Key sources include the [Law of Property Act 1925](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo5/15-16/20), the [Land Registration Act 2002](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2002/9) and the [Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/47), together with a substantial body of case law. ### `subjects.Trusts`

Fingerprint: `16958e81906a`

> Trusts is assessed in FLK2. It covers creating an express trust (including the certainties and formalities), resulting and constructive trusts, the powers and duties of trustees, breach of trust and the remedies for it, including tracing. Key statutes include the [Trustee Act 1925](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo5/15-16/19), the [Trustee Act 2000](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/29) and the [Law of Property Act 1925](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo5/15-16/20), which sets out the formalities for some trusts; much of the rest is case law. ### `subjects.Criminal Law`

Fingerprint: `f289c9b46bc0`

> Criminal liability is assessed in FLK2. It covers the general principles (the conduct and mental elements of an offence, causation, parties and inchoate offences), specific offences such as non-fatal offences against the person, homicide, theft, fraud and criminal damage, and defences. Key statutes include the [Offences Against the Person Act 1861](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/24-25/100), the [Theft Act 1968](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/60), the [Criminal Damage Act 1971](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1971/48) and the [Fraud Act 2006](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2006/35). ### `subjects.Criminal Practice`

Fingerprint: `da9a24144bd2`

> Criminal practice is assessed in FLK2. It covers advice at the police station and detention, bail, first hearings, plea and allocation, trial procedure in the magistrates' court and the Crown Court, evidence (including silence and confessions), sentencing and appeals. Key sources include the [Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1984/60) and its Codes of Practice, the [Bail Act 1976](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/63), the [Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/33), the [Sentencing Act 2020](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2020/17) and the [Criminal Procedure Rules](https://www.justice.gov.uk/courts/procedure-rules/criminal).
